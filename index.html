A/B test analysis · Cookie Cats mobile game · 90,189 players

Moving the gate from level 30 to level 40 cost the game returning players

In Cookie Cats, players reach a gate that asks them to wait or pay before they can keep going. The studio tested moving the first gate from level 30 to level 40. This page reads that test and says what to do about it.

A gap this large would show up by chance about 1 time in 640 if the two versions were identical (p = 0.0016, or 0.0031 after allowing for testing two retention measures). Day-1 retention also leaned lower, by −0.59 points, but its interval runs from −1.24 to +0.06, so it cannot rule out no change (p = 0.074).

How the test was set up

90,189 new players were split into two groups. 44,700 saw the gate at level 30 and 45,489 saw it at level 40. For each player the data records whether they opened the game again one day and seven days after installing, and how many rounds they played in the first 14 days.

RetentionThe share of players who come back on a given day after install. Seven-day retention is the main measure here.
95% intervalThe range of true differences the data cannot rule out. If it includes zero, the data cannot rule out no change at all.
p-valueHow often a gap this large would appear by chance if the two versions were really identical. Smaller means less likely to be luck.

Seven-day retention fell by about 0.82 points, and the whole interval sits below zero. Rerunning the comparison by resampling the players (20,000 times) and by shuffling the version labels both gave the same answer.

Where the drop shows up

Seven-day retention difference, by how many rounds a player ended up playing

Gate 40 minus gate 30, in percentage points. Players are grouped by rounds played in their first 14 days.

The loss is concentrated in players who played 30 to 99 rounds, which is the range where players are most likely to be reaching the gate. They returned 2.17 points less often (27.8% against 25.7%). The other groups show no clear change. Rounds played is counted after the gate can change behavior, so read these groups as a clue about where to look next, not as proof. The 30 to 99 round result holds after adjusting for eight comparisons (p = 0.0033).

Could this test see small changes

Chance of detecting a real change in 7-day retention, with this many players

Each group has about 45,100 players. Curve from the standard formula. Dots come from simulating the whole test 4,000 times.

With this many players the test reliably catches changes of about 0.73 points or more, and the observed change of 0.82 points was just above that line. A change of 0.4 points would be missed about two times in three. Catching a 0.5 point change 80% of the time takes about 96,700 players in each group, and a 0.3 point change takes about 268,600.

What could change the answer

  • The groups are slightly uneven. Gate 40 received 50.4% of players instead of 50% (p = 0.0086). A strict team treats anything below 0.001 as a broken test, so this is not an alarm, but it is worth checking in the assignment logs. The dataset has none, so it cannot be explained here.
  • Retention is the only outcome. The data has no revenue or ad views. If a later gate earns enough to offset about 820 fewer returning players per 100,000 installs, the decision could change, and this data cannot say.
  • Only 14 days of play. A seven-day gap may grow, shrink or fade by day 30.
  • One test, one gate position. It says level 40 is worse than level 30. It does not say level 30 is the best place for the gate.

What to test next

  • Track revenue and ad views alongside retention, so the trade-off is visible.
  • Try a gate earlier than level 30, such as level 20, with day-14 and day-30 retention as outcomes.
  • Plan the sample size first. About 96,700 players per group detects a 0.5 point change in 7-day retention.
  • Log when each player is assigned, so the group split can be audited.

How it was done

The plan was fixed before the results were read. Seven-day retention is the main measure, one-day retention is secondary, and rounds played is a check that the later gate did not change how much people play. Players are the unit, each player appears once, and no one was removed. The SQL (DuckDB) builds the player table and the summaries. Python runs the tests, a two-proportion z-test for retention, resampling for the intervals, a rank test for rounds, Holm adjustment for several comparisons, and a simulation for power. A separate script recomputes every figure here with different code and a shuffling test, and all 45 comparisons matched.

Data is the public Cookie Cats A/B test dataset on Kaggle (Mobile Games A/B Testing). Check the dataset page for its licence. This is an analysis of a past experiment, not a test run by the author.
